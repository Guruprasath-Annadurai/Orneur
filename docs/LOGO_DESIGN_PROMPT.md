# Orca — Brand Mark Design Brief

**To**: Lead identity designer
**From**: Founder/CEO
**Brief type**: Primary mark + full identity system, monochrome only
**Level expected**: this needs to hold up as a trademark-grade identity
system — the kind of mark that still works engraved on metal, printed in
one Pantone ink, or reduced to a 12px favicon with zero loss of meaning.
Treat this as you would a mark for a serious infrastructure or financial
company, not a consumer app icon.

---

## Why monochrome, non-negotiably

No color, anywhere, in the primary mark. Not "mostly monochrome with one
accent" — genuinely tonal only (black, white, and a controlled greyscale
value range between them for any secondary/marketing rendering). Three
reasons, stated plainly so this isn't treated as an arbitrary constraint:

1. **The product's whole positioning is restraint and precision** — a
   platform that shows its work, verifies before it answers, and doesn't
   oversell its own capability. A colorful, "friendly AI" logo would
   directly contradict the product experience it's supposed to represent.
2. **Monochrome forces the concept to survive on form alone.** Color is
   the easiest way to make a weak mark look finished — remove it, and any
   conceptual or constructional weakness becomes immediately visible.
   That's the actual test this mark needs to pass.
3. **Reproducibility.** A monochrome mark works everywhere — single-ink
   letterpress, laser engraving, a fax header, a favicon, a t-shirt print,
   a dark-mode UI — without a single derivative asset needing to be made.
   That's not a nice-to-have at this stage; it's the whole point of
   choosing monochrome now rather than retrofitting it later.

---

## The concept: don't illustrate the idea, construct it

Most logo work at a junior level treats "concept" as *subject matter plus
decoration* — draw the animal, then add a symbolic flourish next to it.
That is explicitly not what's being asked for here. The brief is to
**unify two meanings into one continuous form**, using figure-ground
relationships and negative space the way the FedEx arrow, the WWF panda,
or the Unilever mark do — where a second reading emerges from the
construction of the first, rather than sitting beside it.

**The two meanings that need to become one shape:**

1. **The orca** — an animal that hunts and navigates via active
   verification (echolocation), not instinct or guesswork. This is the
   entire reason the company is named Orca, not a decorative choice.
2. **A verification signal** — a sonar ping, waveform, or return-echo —
   representing the product's real, load-bearing differentiator: it
   checks its answers against real sources before presenting them, rather
   than generating confidently and hoping.

**The construction problem to solve, specifically**: do not draw an orca
silhouette and then place a separate arc or waveform element next to or
behind it. Instead, find the point in the orca's own contour — most likely
the dorsal fin's leading edge, the back-to-tail curve, or the line where
head meets melon — where a modulation of that SAME line can simultaneously
read as a sound wave or sonar return. Candidate directions to explore
(pick the strongest, don't force all three into one mark):

- **Waveform-as-spine**: the dorsal ridge line, instead of a single smooth
  curve, is built from a small number of amplitude variations — reading
  as "orca's back" at a glance, and as an audio/sonar waveform on closer
  inspection. This must remain a single weight, unbroken stroke — the
  waveform reads through modulation of the curve's rhythm, not through
  adding a separate wavy line.
- **Negative-space ping**: the counter-space enclosed between the dorsal
  fin and the body — the "hole" a simplified fin silhouette naturally
  creates — is shaped so that negative space itself resolves into a
  clean, radiused arc segment (a partial ring), rather than an arbitrary
  organic gap. The viewer sees "orca," then notices the sonar-ring shape
  living in the space the fin's negative area was going to have anyway.
- **Echo-tail**: the tail fluke's trailing edge is redrawn as a set of
  parallel, decreasing-amplitude arcs (like ripples/an echo returning) —
  used only if it can be done as a genuine extension of the tail's own
  natural silhouette, not an applied pattern.

Whichever direction is strongest, the test for success is this: **if you
covered the "sonar" reading and only showed the shape to someone cold,
they'd say "orca." If you then pointed out the second reading, they
should immediately see it and be unable to un-see it.** That's the bar —
not "an orca with a sonar icon attached."

## Construction discipline

- **Single, constant stroke weight throughout** (or, if going with a
  filled/solid silhouette approach instead of outline, a single flat
  value with no internal linework) — no varying line weight for false
  depth, no faux-3D bevel, no drop shadow, no gradient fill standing in
  for form.
- **Build on a real modular grid** — circles, a consistent angle system,
  and a defined base unit, the way the London Underground roundel, the
  Bauhaus-derived corporate marks of the 1960s-70s (Massimo Vignelli,
  Paul Rand's era), or Munich 1972's Otl Aicher pictograms were
  constructed. Document the grid — this mark needs a construction sheet
  showing the underlying geometry, not just a finished illustration.
  This also matters practically: a grid-constructed mark can be redrawn
  precisely at any scale without "eyeballing" it back into shape.
- **Optical correction over mathematical purity** — where the literal
  grid-true version looks visually wrong (a curve that reads as heavier
  than it should at a junction, a fin tip that looks blunt at true
  geometric length), correct it by eye. A veteran identity designer knows
  the grid is a discipline, not a cage.
- **Stroke-weight-at-small-size compensation**: the version intended for
  16-24px reproduction may need a very slightly heavier relative stroke
  weight than the large-format version, to compensate for how thin lines
  optically disappear at small sizes on a screen. Deliver both a "detail"
  master and a "small-size" master if the concept requires it — don't
  force one weight to serve both contexts if it genuinely can't.
- **Trademark differentiation pass, required before final**: search
  existing marine-animal logos (WWF's panda aside, check any orca/whale
  marks already in use — SeaWorld's historical branding, various marine
  conservation orgs, any AI/tech company that's used a whale motif) and
  confirm this mark is genuinely distinguishable in silhouette alone, not
  just in the sonar detail. A mark that only differentiates via a
  secondary detail is not trademark-defensible on the primary silhouette.

## Deliverable system (what "done" actually includes)

A single finished PNG is not a deliverable at this level. Expect/produce:

1. **Construction sheet** — the underlying grid, angles, and modular units
   the mark is built from, annotated.
2. **Primary mark** — full detail version, for large-format/print use.
3. **Small-size optimized version** — the same mark, optically adjusted
   for correct reproduction at 16-32px (see stroke-weight note above).
4. **Grayscale value study** — if any tonal variation is used at all
   (e.g., for the marketing/hero rendering), a documented value scale
   (e.g., 100% black / 70% / 40% / white) — not arbitrary opacity.
5. **Clear space and minimum size rules** — the standard identity-system
   deliverables: how much empty space must surround the mark, and the
   absolute minimum size it may be reproduced at before switching to a
   simplified/wordmark-only lockup.
6. **Reversed (white-on-black) and inverse (black-on-white) versions** —
   both fully resolved, not just a naive color-flip if the construction
   doesn't hold up under inversion.
7. **Single-ink/engraving proof concept** — a rendering showing the mark
   as it would appear laser-etched, letterpressed, or foil-stamped in one
   color, to confirm it survives with zero color or gradient crutch.
8. **Wordmark + lockup** — geometric sans-serif, tracked-out uppercase
   "ORCA," paired against the mark's optical (not bounding-box) center.
   Provide horizontal (mark-left, wordmark-right) and stacked (mark-above,
   wordmark-below) lockups.

---

## What to explicitly avoid

- An aggressive, toothy, "predator" orca — wrong tone for an enterprise
  trust product.
- A cute, rounded, mascot-style orca — undermines the precision-instrument
  positioning of the product itself.
- A literal ocean/wave/splash scene — overused in marine-themed logos and
  conceptually irrelevant (this brand is about verification and depth of
  reasoning, not water as a visual theme).
- Any generic "AI startup" visual cliché — neural-net node-and-line
  diagrams, particle swarms, gradient blobs, abstract sparks/orbs.
- Treating the sonar/echolocation idea as a bolt-on badge or corner icon
  rather than something constructed INTO the mark's own line work.
- Any color at all in the primary mark, including "just one subtle accent."

## Starting-point generation prompts (exploration only, not a substitute for the construction process above)

These are useful for rapid concept exploration with an AI image tool, but
the actual deliverable must go through the construction/grid discipline
above — do not treat a generated image as a finished mark.

**Direction A — waveform-as-spine:**
```
A single-stroke, monochrome, minimalist logo mark: an orca (killer whale)
in side profile, silhouette reduced to essential geometric form, where
the dorsal ridge line (the curve from the top of the head along the back
to the tail) is constructed as a subtle audio-waveform / sonar-return
rhythm rather than a smooth arc — small, rhythmic amplitude variations
integrated seamlessly into the single continuous line, not a separate
wavy line added beside it. Pure white line on pure black background, flat
vector style, no gradient, no drop shadow, no 3D, no color. Built on a
visible circular/modular grid logic. Must read clearly as "orca" from a
distance and reveal the waveform rhythm on closer inspection. Style
reference: 1970s Olympic-pictogram-era corporate identity design (Otl
Aicher, Paul Rand), not a modern mascot logo.
```

**Direction B — negative-space ping:**
```
A single-stroke, monochrome, minimalist logo mark: an orca (killer whale)
in side profile, geometrically simplified. The negative space enclosed
between the dorsal fin and the body curve is deliberately shaped into a
clean radiused partial-ring (sonar-ping) form, so the counter-space itself
reads as a second symbol once noticed, rather than an arbitrary gap. Pure
white line on pure black background, flat vector, no gradient, no shadow,
no 3D, no color. Constructed on a visible modular/circular grid. Must
read unambiguously as "orca" first, with the sonar-ring negative space as
a deliberate secondary discovery, not a decorative addition. Style
reference: mid-century modernist corporate mark design, FedEx-arrow-style
figure-ground construction technique applied to a marine-animal subject.
```
